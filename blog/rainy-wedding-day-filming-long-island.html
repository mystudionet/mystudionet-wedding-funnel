Filming a Wedding in the Rain: What Actually Happens and How to Plan for It

By Kursad Yonet · October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Rain on a wedding day does not ruin a wedding film. It changes it. Some of the most striking footage from Mystudionet Productions weddings has come on rainy days, when covered walkways, wet stone and overcast light gave the cinematography a quality that sunshine rarely produces on its own. The key is having a clear contingency plan before the day arrives so no important moment gets caught unprepared when the weather shifts.

I have filmed 300+ weddings across Long Island, from the South Shore to the East End, and a meaningful number of them have had some rain in the forecast. Some had a lot of rain. Couples call me in a panic the week before when the weather app shows clouds and umbrellas. My honest answer is always the same: the rain is not the problem. The lack of a plan is the problem.

What does rain actually look like on camera at a Long Island wedding?

Often better than couples expect. Overcast light is diffused and even. It does not produce the harsh midday shadows that show up on faces and white dresses under direct August sun. Wet stone at a North Shore manor, rain on a wooden dock at a South Shore waterfront venue, the gray shimmer of a covered terrace in Nassau County on a wet October afternoon: these are not problems for a camera. They are texture. They are light doing something real.

Rain also makes people behave differently, and documentary filmmaking depends on authentic behavior. Couples who are laughing under an umbrella, guests huddled under an awning, the blur of drops on a window with the ceremony in the background: that is footage that does not require any staging from me. It just needs someone present to catch it.

What I do not want is for a couple to spend their wedding day looking miserable about the weather. The film reflects the day, and if the day feels ruined, the film will too. A couple who can look at each other in the rain and actually find it funny produces a better film than a couple who spent the whole day apologizing to their guests about the forecast.

What moments are most at risk on a rainy day, and how do we protect them?

Outdoor portraits are the most vulnerable. If the ceremony is inside and the reception is inside, the only moment that genuinely depends on the weather is the couple's portrait session. This is where having a fallback location identified in advance makes the difference. Most venues in Suffolk County, Nassau County and the Hamptons have at least one covered outdoor option, a colonnade, a covered porch, a heated tent or a ballroom with large windows that can work for portraits if the grounds are underwater.

I scout this before every wedding. I ask the venue coordinator what they have used in past rainy day events. I ask the couple if they have seen any spots at the venue they love. And when the day arrives, I have two or three setups already in mind so we are not burning portrait time making decisions in real time.

The other moment that needs attention is the outdoor ceremony, if there is one. Many Long Island venues have a covered outdoor option or a tent that can work well on camera. If the ceremony is moving indoors because of rain, I want to know about that decision as early as possible so I can adjust camera positions. An indoor ceremony at a venue I have not scouted indoors is a fixable problem, but only if I have a few minutes to prepare, not if I find out when the processional music starts.

How does rain change the wedding day timeline?

Usually it compresses the outdoor time and occasionally stretches the indoor getting-ready coverage. When there is rain in the forecast, I always recommend building a buffer into the portrait window, 20 to 30 extra minutes, so there is room to wait for a break in the weather if one comes. On Long Island, a squall will often pass in 20 minutes and leave behind clean, dramatic light. If you have the time in your schedule to wait, sometimes the best portraits of the day happen right after a clearing storm.

What I tell couples is this: do not shrink the portrait time when it rains. That is the natural instinct, to rush or skip it because conditions are imperfect. The instinct is wrong. The portraits are still important. They just might happen under an umbrella or in a covered loggia instead of on a sunny terrace. The coverage and the time spent are the same. The location adapts.

What should couples actually do when rain is in the forecast?

Contact your photographer and filmmaker at least a few days before the wedding. Not the morning of. A few days before, when there is still time to think clearly and make decisions without the pressure of guests arriving in two hours. Walk through the plan together. Identify which parts of the day have indoor alternatives, which moments are fixed outdoors regardless of weather, and what the portrait backup location is.

That conversation takes 20 minutes and it changes everything about how the day feels. Couples who have that conversation show up on a rainy wedding morning already knowing the plan. Couples who do not have it spend the morning trying to coordinate four vendors in a group text while getting their hair done.

I have covered rainy weddings on the East End, at Hamptons venues in the fall, at North Shore properties in spring, and at Nassau County ballrooms that lost their garden ceremony to a thunderstorm. In my experience, the couples who ended up with films they love from those days were not the ones who had perfect weather. They were the ones who were prepared and who chose to have a good time anyway. The camera picks up on that choice. It always does.

With packages starting at $3,500 for 8 hours of coverage and premium options extending to 12 hours, there is time built into any Mystudionet Productions day to adapt to what the weather brings. The plan just needs to be made before the rain arrives.

Frequently asked questions

Can we still do outdoor portraits if it rains on our wedding day?

Often yes, depending on the rain. A light drizzle with umbrellas and a covered porch or colonnade gives a filmmaker interesting material to work with. Heavy rain usually moves portraits indoors, but with professional lighting and a well-chosen interior space, the results can be striking. The key is flexibility in the timeline so there is room to shift when conditions change.

Will rain ruin the outdoor ceremony footage?

Not necessarily. Many Long Island venues have covered outdoor ceremony spaces, pergolas, or tented options that work well on camera. If the ceremony moves fully indoors due to weather, the footage adapts accordingly. A filmmaker who has worked across Suffolk County, Nassau County and the Hamptons has seen this shift many times and plans for both scenarios in advance.

Does rain affect the quality of the wedding film?

Rain changes the look of a film, it does not degrade it. Overcast light is actually softer and more even than direct sun, which can create harsh shadows on faces and clothing. Wet surfaces reflect light in interesting ways. Rain adds texture and movement to exterior shots. Some of the strongest wedding films from Long Island were shot on gray, wet days. The final result depends much more on the filmmaker's preparation than on the weather.

What should couples do when rain is in the forecast for their wedding day?

Contact your photographer and filmmaker at least a few days before to walk through the contingency plan together. Identify which parts of the day have indoor alternatives, which moments are fixed outdoors regardless of weather, and how the portrait time will be handled if conditions are poor. Having that conversation before the day means you are not making those decisions under pressure while guests are arriving.
