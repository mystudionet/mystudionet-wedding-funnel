The First Look: Pros, Cons and What It Looks Like on Camera

By Kursad Yonet · November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

A first look is a planned private moment, usually 60 to 90 minutes before the ceremony, where you and your partner see each other in your wedding attire for the first time, filmed from a respectful distance. It tends to produce some of the most honest, unguarded footage of the entire day. The tradeoff is trading your ceremony aisle reaction for that private moment. Neither choice is wrong; it depends on which scene you will want to watch most in ten years.

Every season I get a version of the same question: should we do a first look? It is one of the most debated decisions in modern wedding planning, and the debate gets louder in the wedding film world because the first look is, for a filmmaker, one of the most valuable moments of the day. Let me tell you what I have seen after filming over 300 weddings across Suffolk County, Nassau County, the Hamptons, and New Jersey.

What is a first look, and what does it actually look like on film?

A first look is a planned, private moment, usually 60 to 90 minutes before the ceremony, where you and your partner see each other for the first time in your wedding attire. The photographer and filmmaker position themselves at a distance to capture both reactions without crowding the scene.

On film, a first look almost always looks beautiful. Because it is controlled rather than chaotic, your filmmaker can place you in good light, choose a background with meaning or visual texture, and capture the full arc of the moment: the anticipation, the tap on the shoulder, the turn, the reaction, the first embrace. When it works, it is the kind of scene that makes people cry in the living room years later. I have filmed first looks on a quiet garden path on the North Shore, on a stone terrace in the Hamptons, in an empty ballroom with long beams of morning light, and against the water on the South Shore. Each one felt different, and each one gave the film something a ceremony crowd cannot replicate: stillness.

What are the advantages of a first look for your wedding film?

Here is the honest filmmaker's perspective. A first look is very good for your film and your overall day, for a few connected reasons.

First, it gives you a private emotional beat before the ceremony begins. There are no 150 guests staring at you, no officiant waiting, no musicians watching the clock. It is just the two of you, and that privacy shows on camera. Unguarded reactions are hard to manufacture later.

Second, it frees up couple portrait time before the ceremony. After the first look, you typically spend 20 to 30 minutes doing portraits together while you are still fresh, calm, and in good early light. Without a first look, that portrait window falls between the ceremony and the reception, when you are tired, slightly overwhelmed, and the light is often flat or already gone. On Long Island in late October through February, that matters a lot: golden hour can arrive before 4:30 pm, and couples who do portraits after a 5:00 pm ceremony are shooting in the dark.

Third, the audio is easier. I always mic the ceremony with two microphones, but ceremonies are acoustically complicated. A first look is intimate and quiet, and your partner's exact words, however short or however long, land clearly on camera.

What are the drawbacks of doing a first look?

The main argument against a first look is real, and I will not dismiss it: you lose the aisle moment as a true first reaction.

For some couples, walking down the aisle and seeing their partner for the first time is the central emotional memory they have been holding since they got engaged. That moment, with the music swelling and every person they love watching, carries a weight that a private first look in a garden simply cannot replicate. If that image is vivid in your mind, do not trade it away for the sake of your film. The film will still be great.

There is also a religious or family tradition angle. Some ceremonies, particularly at houses of worship across Nassau County and Suffolk County, are built around the expectation that partners do not see each other beforehand. If that matters to your families or your faith, honor it. I have filmed many beautiful weddings where the first look was the aisle, and the ceremony reaction carried the entire opening of the film.

Does a first look make the ceremony aisle reaction less emotional?

This is the fear I hear most. The honest answer, based on filming this both ways many times: no. Couples who do a first look still cry during the ceremony. The emotions are different rather than lesser. The first look tends to be quiet and inward. The ceremony aisle moment is public, charged by the room, by the music, by every face turning toward you at once. Both can be powerful. They are just different scenes, and they serve different parts of the film.

What I have noticed is that couples who do a first look often seem calmer during the ceremony. They have already had their moment together. The ceremony can breathe. Whether that is a feature or a bug depends on the couple.

How should we decide if a first look is right for us?

Think about what you will want to watch on film five or ten years from now. Close your eyes and picture both scenarios: a quiet, unguarded moment in private before the day truly begins, or the full ceremony aisle experience with everyone watching. One of those images will feel more like you.

If you are still undecided, think about your venue and your season. A late-afternoon fall or winter wedding on Long Island benefits significantly from a first look, because the portrait light before the ceremony is far better than what is left after. A summer wedding with a 6:00 pm ceremony has more flexibility.

Either way, make the choice deliberately rather than by default, tell your filmmaker and photographer early, and build your timeline around it. That one decision shapes how the entire day flows on camera.

Frequently asked questions

Does doing a first look make the ceremony aisle moment less emotional?

Not in my experience. Couples who do a first look still cry during the ceremony. The emotions are different rather than lesser. The first look tends to be quiet and private; the ceremony aisle moment is public and charged with everyone watching. Both can be powerful, and both film beautifully.

When during the day should the first look happen?

Ideally 60 to 90 minutes before the ceremony, after hair and makeup are finished but while natural light is still good. This gives your filmmaker time for the first look itself and then couple portraits right after. On Long Island, the late-morning to early-afternoon window works well for most seasons.

What if our venue does not have a good spot for a first look?

Most Long Island venues have more options than couples realize: a garden path, a side terrace, a hallway with good window light, or an empty ceremony room before guests arrive. I scout the space when I arrive and find the right spot. If the venue is truly bare, we work with what the light and the moment give us.

Can we skip the first look and still get great couple portraits?

Yes, absolutely. Couples who skip the first look do portraits between the ceremony and the reception. The main tradeoff is that this window is often short and rushed, and the light may not be ideal. With careful timeline planning, you can still get beautiful portraits without a first look.
